Why this exists

You write Python. The platform handles the armor.

🧠 What you focus on

  • Your model, your API endpoints, your business logic
  • Reading a request, returning a response
  • Calling the corporate backend when you need data

🛡️ What the platform adds around you

  • Encrypted, mutually-authenticated connections (mTLS)
  • Identity & permission checks on every call (JWT)
  • Strict routing — only approved paths get through

The idea: security and networking are pushed out of your application and into a dedicated layer next to it. Same protection for every service, and your code stays simple.

Concept · 1 of 4

What is Envoy?

Envoy is a proxy — a smart traffic cop that sits in front of an application. Every request goes through Envoy before it reaches your code, and every response goes back out through it.

📥 It inspects

Checks who is calling, whether their token is valid, and which path they want.

🔀 It routes

Sends approved traffic to the right place, and rejects everything else.

🔐 It encrypts

Wraps connections in TLS so traffic can't be read or spoofed in transit.

Analogy: Envoy is the building's security desk. You're an employee in an office upstairs — you just do your work. Nobody reaches your desk without first passing the guard who checks IDs, logs visitors, and only allows people to the floors they're cleared for.

Concept · 2 of 4

mTLS — both sides prove who they are

Normal HTTPS (TLS): only the server shows a certificate. The client stays anonymous.

Mutual TLS (mTLS): both the client and the server present certificates. Each side verifies the other before a single byte of data flows.

Analogy: a normal website is like a shop — you check the shop is legit, but the shop doesn't check you. mTLS is a secret handshake: both people must know it, or the door stays shut.

  • No valid certificate → connection refused
  • Encrypted end-to-end
  • Stops impostor services from connecting
Concept · 3 of 4

JWT — a signed, tamper-proof ID badge

A JWT (JSON Web Token) is a small token the caller sends with each request. It carries claims — who they are, what they're allowed to do, and when it expires — and it's cryptographically signed.

  • Signature can be verified, so it can't be forged
  • Validation = check signature, expiry, issuer & audience
  • If anything is off → request rejected before reaching you

Analogy: a JWT is a festival wristband. The hologram (signature) proves it's real, the colour says which stages you can enter, and it stops working after the event (expiry). Guards just glance at it — no phone call to HQ needed.

Concept · 4 of 4

The Sidecar pattern

Your component is deployed as two containers side by side: your app and an Envoy "sidecar". All traffic in and out of your app flows through the sidecar first.

  • Your app only ever talks to the sidecar over localhost — no TLS in your code.
  • The sidecar handles everything facing the outside world: certs, tokens, routing.
  • You don't configure it — the platform ships it alongside your container.

Analogy: like a motorcycle sidecar — it rides along carrying the heavy gear (security, certs, routing) so your bike (your app) stays light.

The architecture · End to end

The two players: Gateway and Component

Gateway — the front door for all external traffic Component — your service + its sidecar Corporate backend — internal data/services
Player 1

The Gateway — the single front door

One Envoy gateway sits at the edge. Every external request hits it first. Nothing reaches any component without passing through here.

Step 1

🔐 Terminate mTLS

Verifies the caller's certificate and decrypts the connection. No cert, no entry.

Step 2

🪪 Validate JWT

Checks the token's signature, expiry, issuer and audience. Bad token → 401.

Step 3

🔀 Route to component

Matches the path against an explicit allow-list and forwards to the right service over a fresh mTLS connection.

In short: the gateway is the main reception of the building. It confirms your identity (mTLS), checks your badge and clearance (JWT), then escorts you only to the office you're authorised for (routing).

Player 2

The Component — your service, guarded again

Even though the gateway already checked the request, your component's sidecar checks it a second time. That's zero-trust: never assume an upstream did its job.

🛡️ Sidecar responsibilities

  • mTLS with the gateway — proves traffic really came from it
  • VRE re-validation of the JWT — independent token check
  • Allow only the explicitly defined routes
  • Block / and anything not on the list

🐍 Your Python app

  • Receives a clean, already-verified request on localhost
  • Runs your logic / model and returns a response
  • When it needs corporate data, it calls out through the sidecar (egress)

VRE = the second checkpoint. Like a concert where reception scans your ticket at the street, and the usher scans it again at the door of your specific hall. Two independent checks, much harder to slip past.

Key rule

Only defined routes. No bare /.

The sidecar has an explicit allow-list of paths. A request only reaches your app if its path is on that list. Everything else is rejected at the proxy.

✅ Allowed (example)

  • POST /v1/predict
  • GET /v1/health
  • POST /v1/embeddings

⛔ Blocked

  • / — no catch-all root
  • /admin, /debug, /v1/secret — not declared
  • Any path not explicitly added

Why no /? A wildcard root would expose every path by accident. Listing each route means nothing is reachable unless someone deliberately opened it — the safe default is "closed".

Reading a config

Envoy's building blocks

Every Envoy config is the same handful of nested "resources". Once you can spot these five, any config — gateway or sidecar — reads the same way.

listener — a port Envoy accepts traffic on
transport_socket
TLS / mTLS settings for this port — certs & whether a client cert is required
filter_chain → http_connection_manager
the HTTP engine; holds the two pieces below
http_filters
the processing pipeline: jwt_authn, then router …
route_config → routes
path → cluster matching (your allow-list)
clusters — the destinations Envoy can forward to
load_assignment
addresses of the upstream (your app, a backend, the IdP)
transport_socket
TLS / mTLS used when Envoy connects out to that destination

Hotel analogy: a listener is a guarded entrance, its transport_socket is the ID check at that door, http_filters are the checkpoints you walk through inside, routes are the floor directory, and clusters are the actual rooms (and the secure line out to other buildings).

  • Inbound security lives on the listener's transport_socket.
  • Outbound security lives on the cluster's transport_socket.
  • Auth logic always sits in http_filters; access rules in routes.

Config example gateway-envoy.yaml

What the gateway config looks like

# (1) downstream mTLS — caller MUST show a cert
transport_socket:
  typed_config:
    "@type": ...DownstreamTlsContext
    require_client_certificate: true
    common_tls_context:
      tls_certificates: [gateway.crt / .key]
      validation_context:
        trusted_ca: client-ca.crt

# (2) JWT validation filter
http_filters:
- name: envoy.filters.http.jwt_authn
  typed_config:
    providers:
      corporate_idp:
        issuer: "https://idp.corp.example.com"
        audiences: ["ai-platform"]
        remote_jwks: { http_uri: .../jwks.json }
    rules:
    - match: { prefix: "/v1/" }
      requires: { provider_name: corporate_idp }

# (3) routing — explicit paths only
routes:
- match: { prefix: "/v1/predict" }
  route: { cluster: component_predict }
- match: { prefix: "/" }
  direct_response: { status: 404 }  # no catch-all
  • require_client_certificate: true is the single line that turns TLS into mutual TLS.
  • The jwt_authn filter checks issuer, audience & signature against the IdP's published keys (JWKS).
  • Routes list the allowed prefixes; the final / rule returns 404 instead of forwarding.
  • An upstream cluster (not shown here) connects to the component over mTLS again.
Config example component-envoy.yaml

What the sidecar config looks like

# INGRESS — only the gateway may connect (mTLS)
transport_socket:
  typed_config:
    "@type": ...DownstreamTlsContext
    require_client_certificate: true
    validation_context: { trusted_ca: mesh-ca.crt }

# VRE — re-validate the JWT independently
- name: envoy.filters.http.jwt_authn
  providers:
    corporate_idp:
      issuer: "https://idp.corp.example.com"
      forward_payload_header: "x-jwt-claims" # app reads identity

# route allow-list — exact paths, NO bare "/"
routes:
- match: { path: "/v1/predict" }
  route: { cluster: local_app }      # 127.0.0.1:8080
- match: { prefix: "/" }
  direct_response: { status: 403 }     # everything else blocked

# EGRESS — app calls localhost, sidecar adds mTLS
clusters:
- name: corporate_backend
  transport_socket:
    typed_config: { "@type": ...UpstreamTlsContext,
      tls_certificates: [component.crt / .key] }
  • Second mTLS + second JWT check — the sidecar trusts nothing from upstream.
  • forward_payload_header hands your Python app the verified claims as a header.
  • Routes use exact path matches; the trailing / rule returns 403.
  • Egress: your app posts to localhost:15001; the sidecar attaches the cert and opens mTLS to the backend.
Outbound

Egress — calling the corporate backend

When your app needs internal data, it doesn't connect to the backend directly. It sends the call to its sidecar, which performs the secure egress on your behalf.

What the sidecar does on the way out

  • Opens an mTLS connection to the corporate backend
  • Presents the component's identity certificate
  • Restricts egress to approved destinations only

What you write in Python

requests.post("http://localhost:PORT/backend/...")

You call a plain local address. The sidecar adds the certificates, encryption and identity. No TLS code in your app.

Analogy: the sidecar is your company courier. You hand it a sealed envelope; it knows the secure route, shows the right credentials at the backend's door, and brings the reply back to your desk.

Config example component-envoy.yaml · egress

What the egress config looks like

# 1) a local listener the app posts to (localhost only)
- name: egress
  address:
    socket_address: { address: 127.0.0.1, port_value: 15001 }
  filter_chains:
  - filters:
    - name: envoy.filters.network.http_connection_manager
      typed_config:
        route_config:
          virtual_hosts:
          - name: backend
            domains: ["*"]
            routes:
            - match: { prefix: "/" }
              route: { cluster: corporate_backend }  # only this dest
        http_filters:
        - name: envoy.filters.http.router

# 2) the destination cluster — sidecar adds mTLS going out
clusters:
- name: corporate_backend
  type: STRICT_DNS
  load_assignment:
    endpoints: [{ socket_address:
      { address: api.backend.corp.example.com, port_value: 443 } }]
  transport_socket:
    typed_config:
      "@type": ...UpstreamTlsContext
      common_tls_context:
        tls_certificates: [component.crt / .key]  # our identity
        validation_context: { trusted_ca: corp-ca.crt }
  • The egress listener is bound to 127.0.0.1 — only your own app can reach it, never the outside world.
  • The single corporate_backend cluster is the only allowed destination; anything else has no route.
  • UpstreamTlsContext attaches the component's cert, so the backend sees a trusted, authenticated caller (mTLS).

Chatbot specifics

WebSockets — the chat stream

A chatbot keeps a long-lived WebSocket open instead of one request per message. Envoy blocks protocol upgrades by default, so both the gateway and the sidecar must explicitly allow them.

What "upgrade" means: a WebSocket starts as an ordinary HTTP request carrying Connection: Upgrade · Upgrade: websocket. The server answers 101 Switching Protocols and the same connection switches from HTTP into a two-way stream. upgrade_configs is simply Envoy's opt-in that permits this handshake — it's named after the HTTP "Upgrade" it allows.

# in the http_connection_manager (gateway AND sidecar)
upgrade_configs:
- upgrade_type: websocket      # allow the HTTP -> WS upgrade
stream_idle_timeout: 0s     # keep idle sockets open

# per-route, on the chat endpoint
- match: { prefix: "/v1/chat" }
  route:
    cluster: local_app
    timeout: 0s             # never time out a live chat
    upgrade_configs:
    - upgrade_type: websocket
  • JWT is still checked — the upgrade handshake is an HTTP request carrying the token, so jwt_authn validates it before the socket opens.
  • mTLS is unchanged — the WebSocket runs inside the same encrypted, mutually-authenticated connection.
  • timeout: 0s and stream_idle_timeout: 0s stop Envoy from dropping a quiet conversation mid-session.
  • Without upgrade_configs the handshake is rejected and the chat never connects.

In short: the security model doesn't change for chat — you just tell Envoy "this route may upgrade to a WebSocket, and don't hang up when it goes quiet."

For you, the developer

What you actually have to do

✅ Your job

  • Declare your routes (the paths the platform should expose)
  • Listen on the local port the platform tells you
  • Read the user identity from the forwarded JWT/headers if you need it
  • Call the backend via localhost through the sidecar

🚫 Not your job

  • Writing TLS / certificate handling
  • Verifying JWT signatures yourself
  • Managing mTLS handshakes
  • Configuring Envoy (the platform owns it)

Bottom line: if your code works against localhost and only the declared routes, the gateway and sidecar take care of the rest — encryption, identity, and access control come "for free".

Debugging

Common gotchas & what they mean

401 Unauthorized

Missing, expired or invalid JWT. The gateway or sidecar rejected it — the request never reached your app.

403 / 404 on a path

The route isn't on the allow-list. Add it to your declared routes (remember: no bare /).

mTLS / handshake errors

Certificate problem between hops — usually a platform/cert issue, not your code. Escalate with the trace ID.

Backend call fails locally

Check you're calling the sidecar's local egress address, and that the destination is on the approved egress list.

  • Tip: a request that "never arrives" was almost always stopped at a proxy — check the gateway/sidecar logs first, not your app.
Cheat sheet

The whole thing in five lines

  • Gateway = single front door: terminates mTLS, validates the JWT, routes to components.
  • Component = your app + an Envoy sidecar that re-checks everything (mTLS + VRE JWT).
  • Only declared routes are reachable — never a bare /.
  • Egress to the corporate backend goes out through the sidecar over mTLS.
  • You write plain Python against localhost; the platform supplies the security.

Questions? The platform team owns the Envoy config — you own the routes and the logic.
